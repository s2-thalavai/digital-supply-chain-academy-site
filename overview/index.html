Foundations

What is supply chain management?

Supply chain management (SCM) is the coordination of the flow of goods, information and money from raw-material suppliers to end customers, so that the right product arrives in the right quantity, at the right time and place, at the right cost.

SCM covers designing, planning, executing, controlling and monitoring every activity that moves a product from supplier to consumer. It joins procurement, production, logistics, inventory and distribution into one system, and it manages the information and financial flows that travel with the goods.

How goods, information and money move

Press play to animate the flows, pick one flow to isolate it, or click a node for its role and documents.

Three flows

Goods

Downstream

Raw materials → components → finished goods → customer, and returns back upstream.

Information

Both ways

Forecasts, orders, shipment notices, inventory levels, tracking and invoices.

Money

Upstream

Customer payments flow back through retailers, distributors and manufacturers to suppliers.

Components

Suppliers

Provide raw materials, components and services.

Manufacturers

Turn inputs into finished goods.

Warehouses & DCs

Store inventory and move it on to stores or customers.

Retailers

Sell finished products to consumers.

Customers

Create the demand that drives every other decision.

Logistics & transport

Move goods between every node on time and at the right cost.

Information systems

Track orders, inventory and shipments so people can decide in real time.

Core processes

Demand planning & forecasting

Predict demand so production and inventory match it.

Sourcing & procurement

Select suppliers, negotiate contracts, manage relationships.

Production & manufacturing

Convert materials into finished products efficiently.

Inventory management

Balance supply and demand while keeping holding cost low.

Distribution & delivery

Coordinate transport, warehousing and fulfilment.

Reverse logistics

Handle returns, repair, recycling and disposal.

SCOR model

Plan

Forecast demand, plan capacity, inventory and budgets.

Source

Select suppliers, purchase, receive raw materials.

Make

Produce, assemble, test and package.

Deliver

Warehouse, process orders, ship and deliver the last mile.

Return

Reverse logistics for defective, excess or end-of-life goods.

SCOR Digital Standard: Orchestrate → Plan → Order → Source → Transform → Fulfill → Return

Key activities

Demand forecasting
Predict customer demand from history and trends.
Order processing
Record, approve and process customer orders.
Supplier relationship management
Keep stable supply and better pricing through strong partners.
Transportation management
Choose modes and routes for cost-effective delivery.
Inventory control
Avoid both excess and shortage.
Information flow management
Give every party the same timely view.
Performance monitoring
Track KPIs and find what to improve.

Why it matters

Lower cost
Less waste, better buying, leaner inventory and transport.
Faster delivery
Shorter lead times and reliable promises.
Happier customers
Products available when and where they are wanted.
Resilience
Ability to absorb disruptions in supply or demand.
Sustainability
Fewer empty miles, less waste, lower emissions.
Financial strength
Less cash tied up in inventory; faster cash-to-cash.

Challenges

Bullwhip effect

Small changes in consumer demand become large swings upstream.

Supplier disruptions

Single sources, natural events, geopolitics and tariffs.

Demand variability

Promotions, seasons and trends make forecasts miss.

Inventory imbalances

Too much of the wrong stock, too little of the right stock.

Rising transport costs

Fuel, capacity shortages and rerouting.

Poor visibility and data quality

Teams cannot act on what they cannot see.

Labour shortages

Warehouses and transport struggle to hire.

ESG pressure

Customers and regulators expect proof of responsible sourcing.

Best practices

  • Build strong, collaborative supplier relationships
  • Maintain real-time, end-to-end visibility
  • Use data to drive decisions
  • Apply lean and agile principles where each fits
  • Diversify suppliers; consider nearshoring
  • Plan for risks with backup routes and safety stock
  • Embed sustainability goals in strategy
  • Decide on cost-to-serve, not only unit price

KPIs

OTIF
On-time, in-full: orders delivered complete by the promised date ÷ all orders.
Inventory turnover
COGS ÷ average inventory value.
Fill rate
Units (or lines) shipped from stock ÷ units ordered.
Order cycle time
Time from order placement to delivery.
Cash-to-cash cycle
Days inventory + days receivable − days payable.

Trends

AI and predictive analytics
Forecast demand and spot disruptions early.
Resilience
Supplier diversification, nearshoring, dual sourcing.

Lower carbon footprint and waste.
Digital twins
Simulate network scenarios before acting.

Warehouse robots, autonomous mobile robots, sortation.
AI agents
Software that monitors, recommends and acts within set limits.

A short history

Ancient trade routes
Silk Road and maritime routes move goods across continents through chains of traders.
Industrial revolution
Factories, railways and standardised parts make mass production and distribution possible.
Early 1900s
Assembly lines and scientific management bring flow and measurement to production.
1950s–60s
The shipping container standardises freight and sharply lowers the cost of trade.
1970s–80s
Japanese manufacturers popularise lean and just-in-time; MRP systems spread.
1980s
The term "supply chain management" enters common business use.
1990s
ERP systems integrate finance, purchasing and inventory; the SCOR model is introduced (1996).
2000s
Globalisation, e-commerce, offshoring and RFID.
2010s
Omnichannel retail, cloud platforms, big data and control towers.
2020s
COVID-19 and shipping crises push resilience; AI, automation and ESG reshape supply chains.

What new joiners find hard

  • Processes are deeply interconnected: a change in one team shows up somewhere else
  • ERP, WMS and TMS screens and terminology can be overwhelming at first
  • Departments and suppliers use different words for the same thing
  • Delays and errors have immediate, visible impact, which creates early pressure

Tips

  • Learn the documents first: PO, ASN, goods receipt, invoice, sales order, shipment
  • Follow one order end to end through every system
  • Keep a glossary of your company's terms next to the generic ones
  • Ask "who owns this data?" before changing anything

SCM is a strategic, multidisciplinary discipline. By integrating planning, sourcing, production, logistics and information, it lets organisations meet demand efficiently, keep costs down and stay competitive in a complex global market.
