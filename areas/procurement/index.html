Supplier Management & Procurement

How organisations decide what to buy, from whom, at what price and on what terms, and how they keep suppliers performing.

The procurement process

Ten stages that run from a need to an audited payment. Placing a purchase order is the middle of it, not the start.

  1. Identify the need. What, how many, by when; check stock, production plans and demand.
  2. Purchase requisition. Formal internal request with specs, quantity, cost centre.
  3. Review and approval. Is it necessary and within budget?
  4. Supplier research and selection. Shortlist on price, quality, delivery, reputation, capacity.
  5. RFQ / RFP. Ask suppliers for prices or proposals.
  6. Evaluation and negotiation. Compare cost, quality and reliability; agree price, payment terms, Incoterms, lead time, returns.
  7. Purchase order and approval. PO number, supplier and buyer details, items, quantity, unit and total price, delivery terms and date, payment terms; approved, sent and acknowledged.
  8. Delivery and inspection. Check right product, quantity, quality, packaging and delivery terms.
  9. Invoice matching and payment. Match invoice to PO and goods receipt (3-way), then pay on terms.
  10. Records and audit. Keep every document for compliance and future reference.

Procurement vs supply chain management

Procurement acquires the inputs; SCM coordinates the whole chain those inputs flow through.

DimensionProcurementSupply chain management
ScopeNeeds, suppliers, negotiation, contracts, purchasingEnd to end: plan, source, make, deliver, return
ObjectiveRight inputs at competitive cost, compliant contracts, strong suppliersOptimise the whole chain for cost, availability, speed and service
Time frameShort to medium term; largely operationalLong term; strategic
ImpactSpend control, supplier reliability, profitabilityCross-functional efficiency, customer experience, resilience, ESG
RelationshipA part of SCMGives procurement the demand signal and context

Cost analysis and negotiation

Understand what a price should be before negotiating what it will be.

  • Price analysis: compare quotes, market indices and history
  • Cost analysis / should-cost: build up materials, labour, overhead and margin
  • Total cost of ownership (TCO): price + freight + duty + quality + inventory + end-of-life
  • Negotiation preparation: know your BATNA (best alternative), the ZOPA (zone of possible agreement) and the trade-offs you can offer (volume, term, payment terms)

Supplier selection and evaluation

Choose suppliers on weighted criteria, then keep scoring them on actual performance.

  • Common criteria: quality, cost, delivery, service (QCDS), plus capacity, financial health, risk, ESG
  • Use a weighted scorecard so the decision is explainable
  • Review scorecards quarterly with the supplier

Supplier development

Investing in a supplier's capability (quality systems, lean, technology, capacity) so they can meet your needs better.

  • Joint improvement plans with measurable targets
  • Training, shared engineers, early involvement in design
  • Most useful for strategic and bottleneck suppliers

Project supply chains: EPC contracts

In engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) contracts, one contractor designs, buys and builds a facility for a fixed price by a fixed date, then hands over a working asset ("turnkey").

  • Benefits: single point of responsibility, price and schedule certainty, risk transfer
  • Drawbacks: risk premium in the price, less owner control, expensive scope changes, heavy dependence on one contractor
  • Use when the owner wants a ready-to-operate asset and certainty matters more than flexibility (power plants, refineries, pipelines, renewables)
  1. Engineering. Feasibility, detailed design, drawings, specifications, schedule and budget.
  2. Procurement. Bidding and buying equipment, materials and subcontractor services, plus logistics.
  3. Construction. Mobilise crews, manage subcontractors, safety and quality, then commission and hand over.
